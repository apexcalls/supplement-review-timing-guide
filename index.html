How to Tell If a Supplement Brand's Reviews Were Written Before the Product Existed

A supplement launches on Monday. By Tuesday it has two hundred five-star reviews. By Friday it has a thousand, all glowing, all detailed, all from verified buyers. The product has been on sale for four days. Something does not add up — and the arithmetic is the clue. Reviews have a timeline, and a timeline that does not make sense is one of the clearest signs that the reviews were not written by real customers.

The first thing to check is the date. A genuine review can only be written after the product has been bought, delivered, and used for long enough to form an opinion. A review dated the same day as the launch, or before it, was written by someone who had not used the product. A flood of reviews in the first week, all positive, all similar in length and tone, is the signature of a review campaign rather than a customer base. Real products accumulate reviews slowly, unevenly, and with a mix of opinions. The pattern of the dates tells you more than the content of any single review.

The second thing to check is the language. Genuine reviews are specific and varied: they mention the taste, the size of the capsules, the delivery time, a question they asked customer service. Fake reviews are vague and interchangeable: "great product", "works amazing", "highly recommend", repeated with small variations across dozens of accounts. They praise the product in general terms because the writer has no specific experience to draw on. When twenty reviews say the same thing in slightly different words, you are reading one review written twenty times.

The third thing to check is the reviewer. Click through to a few reviewer profiles. A real customer has a history: reviews of other products, spread over months or years, on different topics. A fake reviewer has a single review, or a cluster of reviews all posted on the same day for products from the same brand. Some review farms are more careful and build plausible histories, but the economics of fake reviews make depth expensive — most fake accounts are shallow, and a minute of clicking will show it.

The fourth thing to check is the negative reviews — or their absence. No product pleases everyone. A product with a thousand reviews and not a single complaint is not a perfect product; it is a curated one. Negative reviews are not a reason to avoid a product. They are evidence that the review system is real. Read them carefully: a genuine complaint about slow delivery or a broken seal tells you more about the company than a hundred five-star praises. There is also the question of incentives. Some brands offer discounts, free products or entry into a prize draw in exchange for a review. That does not automatically make the reviews fake, but it does make them biased — a customer who has been given something is less likely to criticise. Platforms require disclosure of incentives, and the disclosure is usually buried in small print. A gelatin-based formula review shows how to separate genuine customer experience from incentivised praise when you are weighing a product.

Reviews are useful, but only when they are real. Check the dates, read the language, click the profiles, and look for the complaints. A product with a believable review history — slow growth, mixed opinions, specific details, real reviewers — is worth far more than a product with a thousand perfect scores that appeared overnight.
